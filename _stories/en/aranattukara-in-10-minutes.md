Aranattukara is a ward of Thrissur city, a few kilometres south-west of the Round. Today it looks like any busy neighbourhood. But it has a longer and stranger history than most: it was once Thrissur's port. Here is the story in short. Each section links to a fuller chapter.

## Water country and the *karas*

West of Thrissur lie the *kol* (low paddy wetlands), fields that sit below sea level and turn into a shallow lake for half the year. Aranattukara grew on the dry ground at their edge — a *kara*, as land fit for houses and gardens is called. How it got its full name nobody knows for sure. Was it the "half-*kara*", the land of storerooms (*aras*), the bank of the fort country, or simply a bustling place? Each idea has its supporters.

## The port and Sakthan Thampuran's warehouses

Because the water route from the coast ended here, Aranattukara became the landing for Thrissur's goods. Carts and porters brought produce from the hills and the plains; boats carried it on to Kochi, Kodungallur and Kozhikode. Sakthan Thampuran, who ruled Cochin from 1790 to 1805, made the trade in tobacco, salt and pepper a state business and built *pandikasalas* (warehouses) at the landing. Toll posts (*chunkams*) stood on the roads in. When the railway reached Thrissur and new canals moved the boat traffic elsewhere, the port fell quiet.

## The Tharakans

The Chiramel family, Christian traders and physicians from Palayur, are said to have settled by the landing in the late fifteenth century. Tradition says they saved a Raja of Cochin from the Zamorin and were rewarded with the title "Tharakan" and tax-free land. They ran much of the trade at the landing, leased land to new Christian families, and were honoured by both the Raja and the Pope. Their old house still holds a remarkable collection of antiques.

## The Christian settlement and the church

Around the Tharakans grew a community of St. Thomas Christians. For years they rowed to Pazhuvil for Mass; then in 1796, with Sakthan Thampuran's permission, Aranattukara got its first church. A dispute between old and new families split the parish, and in 1896 a second church rose facing the first. For nearly seventy years the village was divided. In 1964 the parishes were reunited, and in 1973 the new St. Thomas Church was consecrated. It was renovated in 2015.

## Temples and Lalur

Christians were the majority, but never alone. Hindu families lived in Kizhakkeppuram, at Lalur and on the north side, and the book records a long tradition of neighbours sharing in each other's feasts. The Lalur Karthyayani temple, an old *kavu* (grove shrine), sends its goddess to the Thrissur Pooram each year. Lalur also has a Subrahmanya temple, and Kizhakkeppuram an Ayyappa temple.

## Schools

Aranattukara's first schools taught reading, writing and arithmetic in the markets. An English primary class began in 1886. A new English school opened in 1922 and, handed to the parish by the Tharakans, became Tharakans School; it became a high school from 1947–48. The Infant Jesus Girls' School followed, and the ashram at Elthuruth, linked with St. Kuriakose Elias Chavara, grew into a centre of learning nearby.

## Work and industry

The people of Aranattukara were known across the Cochin State as traders — in leather, groceries, hill produce, copra and coconut oil. Local merchants started their own bank in 1919. There was industry too: the Cochin Malleables iron foundry, said to be the largest in South India, alongside pharmaceutical works, foundries, a tile company and well-known bakeries.

## Struggles

Aranattukara has had its share of public battles. In 1933 Gandhiji came to the Mathaipuram colony at Lalur to see a school for Harijan children. In 1936 Thrissur rose against a plan to hand its electricity supply to private companies — an agitation that, the book says, drew the town's Christians into the national movement. And for decades Lalur carried the burden of the city's waste dump. The problem first reached the High Court in 1985, and residents' long protests kept it in the headlines.

## Arts and clubs

Drama troupes, reading rooms, football teams and sports clubs filled the evenings — and, the book suggests, helped heal the old parish quarrel by bringing young people from both sides together.

## Today

The village became a ward of Thrissur, and the bamboo thickets and paddy gave way to houses. Yet some of the old place remains: the grid of market streets, the church, the temples, the trees at the John Mathai Centre, and a local environmental group, PLANT, planting saplings since 2000. This site is an attempt to keep the rest from slipping away.
