Rama Varma, better known as *Sakthan* ("the strong") Thampuran, ruled the kingdom of Cochin from 1790 until he died in 1805. He made Thrissur his seat. His palace at Vadakkechira still stands, and he is credited with giving the Thrissur Pooram its present form.

## The king who took over the trade

For Aranattukara, his reign mattered because of the *kadavu* (boat landing). Until then, the book explains, foreign merchants took most of the profit from Cochin's trade. Sakthan Thampuran made the buying and selling of salt, pepper and tobacco a state business. Government warehouses, called *pandikasalas*, went up near the landing. Goods from the hills and the plains were stored there before going on by boat to Kochi, Kodungallur and Kozhikode.

The records quoted in the book give a sense of the scale: thousands of bundles of tobacco, and rice weighed out at the landing for his officers. Salt for the whole state, the book says, was gathered at Aranattukara. Christian boatmen from the village fetched it from the salt pans near Kozhikode.

## The first church

Tradition says the king was friendly towards the Christian traders who handled much of this business. According to the book, it was his decree that gave land for Aranattukara's first church. That church was consecrated in 1796 in honour of Our Lady of the Holy Rosary. It is the ancestor of today's [St. Thomas Church]({{ '/en/places/st-thomas-church/' | relative_url }}).

## Not a man to cross

Not all the stories about him are gentle. A Thrissur tale retold in the book describes a proud Nambudiri who tried to humble the king with a show of gold at a feast. Later, it is said, the Thampuran had that family's house pulled down. He also took the Vadakkunnathan temple out of the hands of the Brahmin assembly that had sided with his enemy, the Zamorin.

The [warehouse landing]({{ '/en/places/warehouse-landing/' | relative_url }}) has long gone. But the trade he organised is a large part of why Aranattukara grew from a riverside village into a busy market town.
